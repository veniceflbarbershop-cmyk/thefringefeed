**TL;DR:** Ed Walters said a craft hovered over his Gulf Breeze home on November 11, 1987, and he had Polaroids to prove it. A Navy physicist backed him. Hundreds of neighbors reported lights. Then in 1990 the new owner of his old house found a nine inch foam model in the attic that looked a lot like the thing in the pictures. Walters said it was planted. Nobody has settled it since.

## A contractor, a blue beam, and five Polaroids

Gulf Breeze sits on a narrow peninsula across the bay from Pensacola. In the fall of 1987 it was a quiet bedroom town. That changed when Ed Walters, a local building contractor, said he looked out from his home office and saw a glowing, ringed object hovering above the trees.

By his account, a blue beam pinned him in place and lifted him off the ground. He also said he grabbed a Polaroid and shot five frames before it left. Those photos went to the *Gulf Breeze Sentinel*, a small weekly, under the name "Mr. X." Later batches arrived signed "Mr. Ed" and "Jim." Walters didn't publicly admit he was the photographer until 1989.

The story kept growing. Walters described the blue beam returning on December 2, 1987. He said he photographed his wife trying to outrun it in February 1988. On May 1, 1988, at Shoreline Park after midnight, he said he took one more photo and then lost an hour. Over that stretch he logged 19 encounters, 32 photographs, and videotape, and he eventually described being abducted under hypnosis.

## The town that saw it too

What made Gulf Breeze different from a lone guy with a camera was the crowd. After the *Sentinel* ran the photos, both the paper and the Mutual UFO Network (MUFON) started fielding dozens of sighting reports from the area. One witness was a sitting city councilwoman, Brenda Pollak, who said she watched a craft hover over Pensacola Bay while Walters, unknown to her, was photographing it a few blocks away.

MUFON went all in. It brought its 1990 annual symposium to Pensacola. Sightings of lights near the Pensacola Bay Bridge kept coming into 1991, and the group didn't shut down its local hotline until 2000.

## The scientist in Ed's corner

The most serious backing came from Bruce Maccabee, an optical physicist who worked for the Navy and had a long record in UFO research. He wrote a 90 page analysis for MUFON and argued Walters could not have faked pictures that good. His line to UPI in 1989 was blunt: "A professional magician would have a difficult time doing this."

Maccabee pushed for better controls, too. He had Walters shoot with two Polaroid cameras mounted on a bar so the images could be triangulated for distance. On at least one occasion a witness opened sealed film boxes, loaded the cameras, and recorded the film pack serial numbers before a shoot. Walters also sat for a polygraph in February 1988 and passed, with the examiner concluding that Walters believed his photos were real.

## June 10, 1990

Craig Myers, a reporter for the *Pensacola News Journal*, had been digging into the case for a while. On June 10, 1990, the paper ran his story about a model spaceship found in the house at 612 Silverthorn Road, where the Walters family had lived until 1988.

The new resident found it in the attic, tucked under insulation. It was roughly nine inches across, built from four foam plates and a sheet of drafting paper, with a blue gel and an orange paper ring. The drafting paper carried what looked like house plans, and the paper reported the handwriting resembled Walters'. News Journal photographers used the model to recreate some of the famous shots, and the match was close.

Walters refused a new polygraph about the model but signed a sworn statement denying he made it. His explanation was that debunkers, or the government, had planted it to wreck him. Gulf Breeze's own mayor, Ed Gray, had already analyzed the photos and called them fake. He said the model confirmed what he had suspected.

Two more details made it worse. The paper reported that Walters had made at least $200,000 from his 1990 book, *The Gulf Breeze Sightings*, which undercut the claim that he had turned down money. And by 1993, a *News Journal* editorial reported that Tommy Smith, who knew the family, said he had watched Walters fake photos as a practical joke.

## The skeptic's case, at full strength

The skeptic doesn't need the model to make the case. It just makes the case easier.

Look at the pattern. A single witness generates nearly all the hard evidence, over months, and the evidence escalates each time someone raises a doubt. When analysts ask for stereo cameras, stereo photos appear. Robert Nathan at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory suspected double exposures and cut and paste work. The *National Enquirer* had sent him the photos before deciding whether to buy them, and it passed. The Center for UFO Studies, no debunking outfit, flagged unevenly spaced windows on the craft and a waviness in one image that looked like a reflection in water. Its scientific director, Mark Rodeghier, told UPI that outside of MUFON's inner circle, most serious researchers considered Gulf Breeze a hoax.

The neighbors? Once a town is primed by front page photos, lights in the sky over a peninsula next to Eglin Air Force Base, one of the largest military installations in the country, will get reported. Flares and aircraft don't need to be explained one by one to explain the pattern.

The polygraph proves little. An examiner concluding someone believes his own story is not the same as confirming the story, and polygraphs are weak evidence either way. Maccabee's analysis, meanwhile, was tied to the book. Critics reported that Walters paid him a share of the advance to write a chapter endorsing the photos, which raises a fair question about independence.

## The believer's answer

Supporters have a reply to each point, and some of them land.

A planted model is not a crazy idea in a case this heated. The house had changed hands, critics openly wanted the story dead, and the model surfaced right as MUFON was bringing its conference to town. A model also explains only some of the photos. It doesn't account for the double camera shots, the sealed film protocols, or the many residents who reported sightings with no connection to Walters at all. Maccabee's long view was that even with Ed's photos thrown out, the rest of the Gulf Breeze material still deserved study.

## Where it stands

Myers laid out his full case in a 2006 book, *War of the Words*. The *Sentinel* is gone, bought by the *News Journal* and closed. Gulf Breeze still gets visitors who came because of the photos.

What's left is a single question you have to answer for yourself. Is a foam model under the insulation a smoking gun, or a convenient plant in a town where plenty of people wanted one found? The photos are still out there. Look at them next to the model and decide.
