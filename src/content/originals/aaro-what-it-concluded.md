**TL;DR:** The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) says it has found no verifiable evidence of alien technology or a hidden recovery program. Every case it has closed turned out to be something ordinary, mostly balloons, birds, drones, and satellites. But most of its new cases aren't closed at all. They sit in an archive for lack of data. Critics say the office oversold its conclusions and never got the cooperation it needed. Its second historical volume is still missing.

## What AARO is

Congress created AARO in 2022 as the Pentagon's clearinghouse for unidentified anomalous phenomena, or UAP. It replaced the UAP Task Force, which issued its own preliminary report in 2021. AARO has two jobs. It investigates current reports, mostly from military personnel and sensors, and it was ordered to write the official history of the government's involvement with the subject going back to 1945.

## The history report: "no empirical evidence"

In March 2024 AARO released Volume I of its *Report on the Historical Record of U.S. Government Involvement with Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena*. It covered 1945 through October 31, 2023, and walked through Projects Sign, Grudge, and Blue Book up through the Pentagon's AATIP effort and AARO itself.

The headline finding was that AARO found no empirical evidence that any UAP sighting represented extraterrestrial technology. It also said it found nothing to show any information had been illegally withheld from Congress. Claims of secret reverse engineering programs, the report argued, came from people who had partial or unauthorized glimpses of real classified programs that had nothing to do with aliens.

The report did include one genuine surprise. It described Kona Blue, a proposal floated in the 2010s to fund a program for studying recovered craft. It was never approved. AARO said the people pushing it already believed the government was hiding off world technology, and that some of them were behind the accusations that followed.

Volume I was supposed to have a sequel covering interviews and research from November 2023 through April 2024. As of the latest reporting we could find, from September 2026, Volume II still hadn't been published. In February 2026 the Pentagon said it had nothing to announce.

## The case numbers: closed, and not closed

AARO's newest annual report, covering fiscal 2025, finally came out on July 20, 2026. Its data stops at May 30, 2025, so it's a look backward, not a live snapshot.

The office logged 319 new reports in that period. It resolved 114 of them, and every one traced to something ordinary. The list included balloons, birds, satellites, aircraft, and drones, plus one rocket launch and one person in a jet pack. AARO also cleared 256 older cases, for 370 resolutions in total, and 238 of those came from new three dimensional modeling of satellite flares. Its total case holdings stood at 1,870.

The other side of the ledger matters more. 191 of the 319 new reports went into what AARO calls its "active archive," meaning there wasn't enough data to say what they were. That's 60 percent of the new cases. AARO's position is that unresolved doesn't mean anomalous. It means unknown.

The same report flagged at least one case still under active investigation: a Navy observation off the Virginia coast describing roughly 100 airborne objects and two surface craft that were probably uncrewed.

As in every prior report, AARO said none of its resolved cases showed advanced foreign capabilities or breakthrough technology.

## Meanwhile, the files started coming out

AARO's reports now share the stage with something else. In February 2026 President Trump directed the Pentagon, now styled the Department of War, to declassify UAP records. The first batch went up on May 8, 2026, at war.gov/ufo under a program called PURSUE, with about 160 files spanning the 1950s through 2026. By late October there had been seven releases.

AARO helps prepare those files, but PURSUE is not an AARO investigation. A file showing up in the archive marked unresolved doesn't mean anyone has studied it closely.

## The case against AARO

Critics don't mainly argue that AARO is lying about the balloons. They argue it reached sweeping conclusions on a thin record.

Their strongest point is about who talked. Christopher Mellon, a former deputy assistant secretary of defense for intelligence, said at the time of Volume I that many witnesses didn't trust AARO and wouldn't speak to it. David Grusch, the former intelligence officer who told Congress in 2023 about an alleged crash retrieval program, never sat for an AARO interview. AARO's own records show it invited him repeatedly. Researcher Justin Snead points out that Volume I rested on roughly 30 interviews, far too few, he argues, to support a conclusion that no hidden program exists anywhere.

The tone bothered people too. Lue Elizondo, who ran AATIP, called the Volume I report intentionally dishonest and misleading. Others noted it was largely shaped by AARO's first director, Sean Kirkpatrick, who left the job before it was released and kept defending it in public.

Then there's the paperwork. Congress set annual deadlines, and the fiscal 2025 report arrived months past the cycle. Volume II is more than two years overdue. Even AARO's sympathizers find it hard to argue an office is being fully transparent when it can't ship its own required reports. In September 2026 the Pentagon moved to buy access to a large private archive of older civilian case files, which suggests the historical record AARO wrote from was incomplete.

## The case for AARO

AARO's defenders have a simpler story, and it's not a weak one.

Every time the office gets good data, the case closes, and it closes as something ordinary. That's 370 resolutions in one report with no exceptions. The cases that stay open are the ones with a single blurry frame or a missing range measurement, and no investigator anywhere can resolve those.

On the history, AARO did find things. It's been reported that its review turned up a decades old Air Force hazing ritual, in which some personnel were shown fake saucer images and told about a secret recovery program. If true, that's exactly the kind of source that could produce sincere, firsthand sounding testimony about programs that don't exist. Defenders also note that the critics' central evidence is still secondhand. Testimony that someone was told about a program is not the program.

## You decide

Both readings fit the same numbers. Either an office with real sensor access keeps finding mundane answers wherever the data is good, or an office that never got full cooperation declared a negative it couldn't prove. The reports are public at aaro.mil and the raw files are at war.gov/ufo. Read the active archive cases for yourself and see which story they tell you.
