**TL;DR:** From the early 1970s to 1995, U.S. intelligence funded research into remote viewing, the claimed ability to describe distant places with the mind. When the program closed, the CIA asked two outside experts to review the lab results. Statistician Jessica Utts concluded psychic functioning was established. Psychologist Ray Hyman agreed the results weren't chance but said that didn't prove anything paranormal. The official verdict was that remote viewing wasn't useful for spying. The files are now public, so you can read the argument yourself.

## How a psychic program got a security clearance

The work began in 1972 at the Stanford Research Institute, where physicists Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff started testing people who claimed psychic ability. Ingo Swann and Pat Price became early stars. By 1977 the Army had stood up its own small unit at Fort Meade, Maryland, with the Defense Intelligence Agency involved.

The program wore a string of code names over the years, including Gondola Wish, Grill Flame, Center Lane, and Sun Streak, before everything was folded under the name Stargate in 1991. It was never big. Accounts put it at roughly 15 to 20 people, and by the end only three viewers were still on the books, working on about $500,000 a year according to *Time*. Over its life the effort cost around $20 million.

Supporters credit the program with real hits, including help locating a downed Soviet aircraft in 1976. Other claimed successes, involving a submarine, Gulf War Scud missiles, and North Korean plutonium, have never been independently confirmed.

## The 1995 review

In 1995 the program moved to the CIA. The agency hired the American Institutes for Research (AIR) to evaluate two things: whether the laboratory evidence held up, and whether remote viewing had ever produced intelligence anyone could use.

For the science half, AIR picked two reviewers on purpose to represent both sides. Jessica Utts, a statistics professor at UC Davis, had published work open to paranormal interpretations. Ray Hyman, a psychology professor at the University of Oregon, was a longtime skeptic, and he had history here: decades earlier his report on SRI's testing of Uri Geller called Geller a fraud. AIR's own description of the pair was that both were fair and open minded scientists.

They focused mostly on the later experiments run at Science Applications International Corporation (SAIC), where physicist Edwin May led the research after it left SRI.

## What they agreed on

This is the part that usually gets lost. Hyman did not say the data was noise.

Both reviewers agreed the SAIC experiments fixed the sloppiness of the early SRI work. Both agreed the effects were real in the statistical sense. Hyman wrote that the results were too large and consistent to be written off as flukes. And both agreed on the obvious next step, which was to have independent judges, blind to the targets, rescore the existing sessions.

## Where they split

Utts went further. Her report concluded that psychic functioning had been well established, and that the effect showed up at similar strength across different labs.

Hyman's response is worth reading in full, but the core of it fits in one line. In his words, unexplained departures from chance are "a far cry from compelling evidence for anomalous cognition."

His argument ran on several tracks. Ten experiments from one lab, he said, were far too few to build on, and too recent to have been picked apart for hidden flaws. Similar effect sizes across labs don't show a common cause, because an effect size says nothing about where it comes from. And without a positive theory of when psi should appear and when it shouldn't, there's no way to tell a psychic effect from an undiscovered bias. Where Utts saw replication, Hyman saw inconsistency.

## The verdict that actually mattered

AIR's operational review was harsher than either scientist. It found that remote viewing had never produced intelligence that guided an actual operation, and it concluded that continuing to use it was "not warranted." The CIA shut the program and declassified it that year. In January 2017 the agency put the Stargate records online in its CREST reading room.

## The skeptic's case, at full strength

The strongest skeptical argument isn't that the viewers were frauds. It's that the experiments couldn't tell psi from people.

Psychologist David Marks, who studied the SRI work closely, catalogued design problems including the possibility of sensory leakage, meaning cues that let a viewer or judge pick up information through ordinary means. He also pointed to a structural conflict: Edwin May was the principal investigator, and he also served as a judge and gatekeeper for which sessions counted. Even an honest researcher in that position can tilt results without meaning to.

Then there's the problem of judging itself. Remote viewing transcripts tend to be loose descriptions, shapes and textures and impressions. Matching a vague transcript to one of five target photos invites the judge's brain to find connections. Hyman described many of the transcripts as vague and general. A small, consistent edge in those conditions is exactly what you'd expect from subtle bias, which is why both reviewers wanted independent rejudging before anyone drew conclusions. That rejudging, as far as the public record shows, didn't happen before the lights went off.

## The case for taking it seriously

Utts' supporters make a fair point back. Skeptics asked for tighter protocols, got them at SAIC, and the effect didn't disappear. Hyman himself conceded it was too consistent to be chance. If a drug trial produced numbers like these, nobody would demand a theory of the mechanism before accepting that something was happening. The standard being applied to psi, they argue, is one no other field has to meet.

## You make the call

Both full reviews are public. So is the AIR report, along with many of the program's own files. The disagreement between Utts and Hyman was never about the math. It was about how much evidence an extraordinary claim needs before you accept it, and that's a question each reader answers alone.
