**TL;DR:** Since the 1960s, researchers at the University of Virginia School of Medicine have investigated more than 2,500 cases of young children who talk about a previous life. The children usually start between ages two and five and stop by about seven. In the strongest cases, a real person who matches the child's statements has been identified, and some children carry birthmarks that line up with that person's fatal wounds. The best known American case, a Louisiana boy who described being a World War II pilot, is also one of the most disputed. A new study this month adds a strange pattern: people who died violently seem to come back sooner.

## How a psychiatry chairman got into this

In 1960, Ian Stevenson was chairman of the psychiatry department at the University of Virginia. That year he published a review of 44 cases he'd found in the existing literature of people who said they remembered a past life, where the person they described had been identified.

He asked readers to send him more. He wasn't planning to run fieldwork himself, but when he followed up on reports from India, he saw 25 cases in four weeks. A week in Sri Lanka turned up seven more. He realized these claims were far more common than anyone had assumed.

Chester Carlson, the inventor of the technology behind the Xerox copier, read Stevenson's work and offered funding. His money, and later his bequest, let Stevenson set up a small research unit at the medical school. Today it's called the Division of Perceptual Studies, and it's still there.

## What a case looks like

The pattern is consistent across countries. A child, usually between two and five years old, starts talking about another life. The average age when it begins is just under three. The child may name places, people, and especially how they died. Many show strong emotion when they talk about it. Somewhere around age seven, the memories usually fade.

Researchers interview the family, write down what the child said, and then try to find out whether a person matching those details actually lived. When they can identify that person, they interview that family too and check each statement.

Stevenson's successor, child psychiatrist Jim Tucker, spent more than 20 years on these cases and focused on American families, partly to answer the objection that the phenomenon only shows up where reincarnation is already part of the culture. He led the division from 2014 until he retired at the start of 2025.

## Birthmarks

The most physical evidence in the archive involves the body. In some cases, the child was born with a birthmark or birth defect that matched a wound, often a fatal one, on the person whose life they described.

Stevenson collected about two hundred of these in his 1997 work *Reincarnation and Biology*. Where possible he compared the marks with autopsy reports or medical records of the earlier person. He argued that some phobias, strong attractions, unusual abilities, and illnesses in these children couldn't be fully explained by genetics or upbringing.

## The boy who remembered the war

The most famous American case is James Leininger. Starting at about age two, he had intense nightmares about a plane crash. He said he'd been an American pilot shot down by the Japanese. Before anyone had identified who that pilot might be, he talked about a ship called Natoma, a fellow pilot named Jack Larsen, and a crash near Iwo Jima. He signed his drawings "James 3."

His parents eventually identified a match: James M. Huston Jr., a Navy pilot who flew from the escort carrier USS Natoma Bay and was killed on March 3, 1945. He was the second James Huston, which would make a reborn version the third. Tucker investigated the case and reported that about 30 of the boy's statements checked out for Huston. The Leiningers wrote a bestselling book about it, *Soul Survivor*, in 2009.

## This month's study

The research hasn't stopped. In a November 2026 interview with WBUR's *Here & Now*, UVA researcher Saeedeh Sadeghi described a new study suggesting that people who died violently or unexpectedly tend to be reborn sooner than others.

She also said that more than half of the children who remember drowning in a past life are afraid of water, often before they could even talk, and that many of these kids show what look like symptoms of trauma from the death they describe.

## The skeptic's case, at full strength

The serious criticism isn't that these families are lying. It's that memory and storytelling are easier to shape than anyone realizes.

The Leininger case shows how. Philosopher Michael Sudduth reexamined it in 2021 in the *Journal of Scientific Exploration* and argued that the public version is a narrative built backward. When James was about three, his mother contacted Carol Bowman, a past life regression therapist and author, and the family followed her advice, including telling the boy his nightmares were memories of a past life. Sudduth argues that the family may have helped guide the story, without meaning to, and that several key details weren't written down until after the family had started researching Huston. In his view, the case fails as evidence.

The broader version of that argument applies to the whole archive. Most cases come from cultures where reincarnation is expected, and families there may look for a match and then remember the child's words in a way that fits it. Interviews often happen years after the statements. Stevenson's method depended on careful investigators, but it rarely had records made before the previous person was identified.

## The researchers' answer

Tucker answered Sudduth in the same journal in 2022. He pointed out that Bowman's advice was general guidance about past life memories. She knew nothing about Huston. Telling a child he was remembering a past life could plausibly lead him to invent one, Tucker argued, but not one that happened to match the specific end of a specific pilot's life, down to the name of his ship.

And whatever weaknesses individual cases have, the birthmarks are hard to coach. A family can shape a toddler's story. It can't shape the location of a mark the child was born with.

## Decide for yourself

UVA publishes a large share of this work, including Tucker's full Leininger case report and his response to Sudduth. Sudduth's critique is public too. Read both sides of the strongest case and decide whether you think it's the best evidence for reincarnation, or the clearest example of how a story grows.
