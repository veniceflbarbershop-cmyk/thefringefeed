**TL;DR:** The reincarnation idea that grabs most people isn't just "I lived before." It's that something useful comes with you, a lesson, a skill, a warning. Researchers at the University of Virginia have documented children whose fears match how their past self died, children with unexplained strong interests, and a small set of cases where a child reportedly spoke a language nobody taught them. Those same researchers do not use hypnotic past life regression, and they warn that it mostly produces fantasy. If there's a way in, the research points somewhere other than the hypnotist's couch.

## The part of reincarnation that matters most

Ask people who believe in reincarnation why it matters to them and the answer is rarely about the afterlife. It's about this life. The idea that a fear you can't explain has a reason. That a talent you picked up too easily was learned before. That a mistake you keep repeating might be one you've made more than once, and could finally learn from.

That's a testable idea, at least in part. If a person carries something from a previous life, it should show up in behavior, not just in stories. The researchers who've collected the most cases have looked for exactly that.

## Fears that fit the death

The clearest pattern is phobias. In many of the University of Virginia's cases, a child has an intense fear that lines up with how the previous person died.

Earlier this month, UVA researcher Saeedeh Sadeghi told WBUR that more than half the children who remember drowning in a past life are afraid of water, and that for many of them the fear showed up before they could talk. A baby can't be told a drowning story. If the fear came first, it's hard to say the story created it.

Sadeghi also described a number of these children as showing what look like post traumatic stress symptoms tied to the death they describe. That cuts both ways for the hopeful version of reincarnation. Something seems to carry over, but it isn't always wisdom. Sometimes it's an old wound.

## Attractions and abilities

Fear isn't the only thing that seems to travel. Ian Stevenson, who founded the UVA research program, argued that certain phobias, strong attractions, unusual abilities, and even illnesses in these children couldn't be fully explained by genes or by how they were raised. Some children show an intense, early interest in something linked to their previous life, like the boy who remembered being a WWII pilot and was obsessed with airplanes before he could explain why.

Sadeghi added one more observation: the children who describe past lives seem, on average, to have advanced verbal skills. She didn't give comparison data, and it's an open question which way that runs. A highly verbal toddler may simply be more able to put strange experiences into words.

## Speaking a language nobody taught them

The most dramatic claim is xenoglossy, a person speaking a foreign language they never learned. Stevenson split it into two types. Recitative xenoglossy is repeating words or phrases by rote. Responsive xenoglossy is actually holding a conversation in that language, which would be far harder to explain away.

In 2025, UVA researchers Philip Cozzolino, Marieta Pehlivanova, and Jim Tucker went through the division's database of more than 2,200 cases and found 40 that had been coded as involving xenoglossy. They compared those with 872 cases that clearly didn't.

The ordinary explanations didn't fit. Children with xenoglossy didn't start talking earlier, and their parents weren't more educated. What did line up was emotion and trauma. Xenoglossy cases were more likely to involve strong emotion when the child spoke about the past life, and the previous person was far more likely to have died violently. A violent death came with nearly three times the odds.

## The warning about hypnosis

Here's where the research turns against what most people try first. Past life regression, where a hypnotist guides you back to an earlier life, is the most popular route to "remembering." The UVA division says plainly that it has never used hypnotic regression to investigate cases.

The reason is experiments. In 1991, psychologist Nicholas Spanos and colleagues ran four studies in which volunteers were hypnotized and told to go back before birth. Many produced a past life identity. But those lives reflected whatever the hypnotist had suggested, and the participants often got basic historical facts wrong in ways a person from that era never would. Change the instructions, and you changed the past life.

Stevenson himself wrote that hypnotic regression rarely turns up evidence of real memories, and that a strong emotional experience during a session doesn't mean a real life was recovered. The division also notes that feeling better after regression doesn't prove the memory was real. Relief can come from the attention, the story, or the meaning a person draws from it.

## The skeptic's case, at full strength

Put together, a skeptic would say the "carry over" evidence is soft at every point. A fear of water is common in young children. Interest in airplanes is common in small boys. Advanced verbal skill makes a child better at telling vivid stories, true or not. And the authors of the 2025 xenoglossy study were candid about the biggest problem: they had no way to confirm, beyond the original investigators' notes, that the children really spoke the language. Their data are old case files, not recordings.

The emotion and trauma link could even be a sign of storytelling. Dramatic deaths make dramatic stories, and dramatic stories get remembered, retold, and embellished.

## The case for carry-over

The other side has a point the skeptic has to answer. The patterns aren't random. If these were just common childhood fears and fantasies, they shouldn't track how a specific previous person died. Yet the drowning cases cluster around fear of water, and the violent deaths cluster around the strongest cases and the rare language claims. A pre-verbal fear that later turns out to match a death story is not easy to produce by coaching.

## So can you remember, and learn?

The honest read of the research is that if anything carries over, it seems to come early, in childhood, without prompting, and often attached to a hard death. It fades around age seven. The researchers' own advice is that a hypnotist is more likely to hand you a story than to retrieve one.

That doesn't close the door on what believers hope for. It suggests the conditions matter: little suggestion, careful records, and checking details against history before you accept them. If you want to know whether your own unexplained fear or talent has a deeper root, those are the tools the people who study this for a living would use. Weigh the evidence and decide what you make of it.
