**TL;DR:** Traveling into the future isn't speculation. It happens every day in small amounts and has been measured with atomic clocks. Traveling into the past is harder, but general relativity contains solutions that allow it. Stephen Hawking proposed that some unknown law blocks it. Since then, physicists have shown on paper that time travel to the past might avoid the classic paradoxes, and in 2026 a team calculated how much information could be sent backward through one of these loops. Nobody has built a time machine. Nobody has proven one is impossible either.

## Forward is already real

Start with the part that isn't controversial. Under Einstein's relativity, time doesn't tick at the same rate for everyone. It runs slower for things moving fast and for things deep in a gravity well.

Caltech physicist Kip Thorne, who won a Nobel Prize for gravitational wave work, has written that the laws of physics let an advanced enough civilization jump forward in time as fast as it likes. It's been tested in the lab. Atomic clocks on Earth's surface run measurably slower than ones higher up, and the GPS satellites in your phone's navigation have to correct for this every day or they'd drift off target.

So a trip to the future is a matter of engineering. Get close to light speed, or park near a black hole for a while, and you'll come home to a world that aged more than you did.

## Backward is where it gets strange

Going the other way requires something physicists call a closed timelike curve, or CTC.

Your path through space and time is a line, your timelike curve. Gravity bends that line. Bend it hard enough and it can loop back on itself, returning you to a moment you've already passed through. That loop is a CTC, and the uncomfortable fact for anyone who wants to rule out time travel is that general relativity allows them.

They show up in several solutions to Einstein's equations. One of the most cited is the rotating black hole. In that model, the singularity at the center isn't a point but a ring, and closed timelike curves arc around it.

## Hawking's objection

In 1992, Stephen Hawking published a paper in *Physical Review D* called "Chronology Protection Conjecture." His argument was that when you try to build a time machine, quantum effects pile up energy at the critical moment and wreck it before it can form. He suggested that some deeper physics, beyond relativity, stops time travel on anything bigger than the microscopic scale.

He had a memorable way of putting it. He wrote that it seemed as if a Chronology Protection Agency existed to "make the universe safe for historians."

It's worth being clear on what that is. A conjecture is an educated guess, not a proof. Whether quantum gravity really destroys a would-be time machine is still an open question.

## The paradox problem, and the escape hatches

The bigger objection was always logical. If you can go back, you can stop your own birth, and then who went back? That's the grandfather paradox, and it has driven a lot of physicists to assume backward time travel just can't happen.

There are two well known ways out. One, the Novikov self-consistency principle, says the universe only allows histories that hang together, so any trip you take was always part of the timeline. The other came from Oxford physicist David Deutsch, who showed that in a quantum version of a CTC there's always some state a particle can take that makes its trip safe.

In 2011, a team that included MIT's Seth Lloyd simulated a CTC in the lab and sent a photon a fraction of a second back in time, in a simulated sense. They found quantum physics seemed to allow only self-consistent trips, so the grandfather paradox couldn't happen.

Then in 2020, Germain Tobar, an undergraduate at the University of Queensland, and his supervisor Fabio Costa published a paper in *Classical and Quantum Gravity*. They showed mathematically that CTCs can coexist with ordinary cause and effect and with what looks like free choice. Their example was a traveler going back to stop a pandemic's patient zero. In their model, events rearrange so the pandemic still happens and the traveler still has a reason to go back, but the traveler's choices along the way are real. You can act. You just can't break the loop.

## The 2026 paper

This year the idea moved a step further. Kaiyuan Ji, a Cornell graduate student, worked with his advisor Mark Wilde and with Seth Lloyd on a paper in *Physical Review Letters*. Ji has said the project was partly inspired by rewatching the movie *Interstellar*.

Their question was practical in a strange way: if closed timelike curves exist, how much information could you send through one to the past? They worked out the capacity of such a channel and found it can carry more than an ordinary channel. One reason is that the sender in the future remembers what the past looked like, and that memory can help fix errors in the message.

## The skeptic's case, at full strength

Here's the problem every one of these papers shares. They answer the question "if a time loop existed, would it work?" None of them shows a time loop exists.

Giulio Chiribella, a quantum physicist who wasn't involved in the 2026 study, put it plainly: we don't know if these curves exist in our universe. The rotating black hole solution assumes an idealized object, and real black holes may not have the clean interior the math describes. The lab experiments simulate CTCs with separate particles standing in for younger and older versions of the same one. When the Australian photon simulation came out, other physicists argued it did little to show time travel is possible in nature.

And Hawking's point hasn't been knocked down. If quantum gravity really does blow up every attempt to build a loop, then all of this math describes roads that can never be paved. "Not forbidden by relativity" is a long way from "possible."

## The case for keeping the door open

The other side has a fair reply. For a century, the main reason to reject backward time travel was that it produced nonsense. That reason is getting weaker. Multiple independent approaches now show that time loops can be logically consistent, and the latest work treats them as something you could, in principle, compute with.

Physics has a history here, too. Black holes themselves were once thought to be mathematical oddities that nature would never produce. Then we photographed one.

## Where that leaves you

Forward time travel is settled science. Backward time travel is allowed by our best theory of gravity, blocked by nothing we've proven, and doubted by many of the people who know the math best. Read the papers in the sources below and decide for yourself where the burden of proof sits.
